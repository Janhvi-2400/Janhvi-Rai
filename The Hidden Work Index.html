1What I mean by hidden work

Most workflows have a small amount of real work in them and a lot of coordination around it. Someone chases a sign off. Someone looks for the latest version of a file. Someone types the same details into a second system and then waits for another team to reply.

None of that is anyone's job, so it rarely gets measured. I call it hidden work, and it is what this study tries to count.

In this example the invoice spends nine working days in the process, and someone works on it for 52 minutes. That ratio, 1.3%, is called flow efficiency. It is a standard Lean measure and the main number this study collects for every workflow.

Adding AI to a process like this would make some of those 52 minutes faster. It would not touch the nine days unless the waiting changes too.

Research

The study uses four pieces, in this order: a score for friction, a test of whether handoffs drive it, a gate that decides whether AI belongs at all, and a sequence of fixes that puts AI near the end. The worked example at the bottom is synthetic.

The Hidden Work Score

Seven dimensions, each scored 0 to 100 from one or two questions, weighted equally in version 1.0. A higher score means more friction. Equal weights are a starting hypothesis that the pilot will test.

Why equal weights: any other split would be a guess presented as a finding. After the pilot, five practitioners will weight the dimensions independently, and the version history will record whether it changed anyone's result.

Each block is one dimension's contribution: its score divided by seven.

The handoff tax

Every time work changes hands, it joins a queue. Context gets lost, nobody is sure who owns the next step, and someone starts chasing. The study tests whether friction rises with each handoff (hypothesis 1).

What a handoff costs: waiting in a queue, lost context, unclear accountability, rework when information is missing, status chasing, and the same update sent twice.

The AI suitability gate

A workflow can score high on readiness and still fail the gate. Checks run in order, and the first one that fails decides the outcome.

  1. Could removing or merging a step solve the problem?If yes: fix the process first
  2. Is the process stable, and does information arrive in a usable form?If no: fix the process first
  3. Do the decisions follow clear written rules?If yes: automate without AI
  4. Could an error materially affect a person's money, health, rights or access, with no one able to review it?If yes: do not automate
  5. Is sensitive data involved, or could errors affect a person?If yes: AI with human review at most
  6. Can outputs be checked, is one person accountable, is value measurable, and do errors only cost time?If all yes: AI appropriate. Otherwise: AI with human review

Sequence of fixes

Six stages, worked through in order. The principle behind it is old: don't automate a process before you've fixed it. Michael Hammer argued this in the Harvard Business Review in 1990, and it is still the step most often skipped.

Case study Synthetic demonstration · not research data

Supplier invoice approval at a fictional subcontractor

This case was written to show how the method works. No organisation was interviewed. Every number is invented. Real case studies will appear here only with the organisation's written consent.

As Is and To Be

Same lanes, same scale. Highlighted steps are hidden work. Dashed steps are automated.

Root cause

No one owns the workflow end to end. Approval rules depend on the site manager, and purchase orders are not always raised before work is ordered, so invoices can't be matched.

What not to do

Buy an AI invoice processing tool now. It would read the PDFs faster and still wait nine days for approvals that have no rule.

Measurement plan

Median lead time, share of invoices approved automatically, share returned for correction. Baseline in week 0, checks at weeks 8 and 12.

Hidden Work Methodology v1.0

Methodology

Version 1.0 was fixed on 21 October 2026, before any interviews, so the results can't reshape it after the fact. Treat it as a hypothesis. The pilot is there to test it.

Research question

In routine workflows inside Irish organisations, how much of the effort and elapsed time is hidden work, where does it come from, and what should be simplified, standardised, automated or augmented with AI first? The study makes claims about the workflows it studied. It does not estimate a national figure.

Sample and workflow selection

One workflow per organisation. A pilot of 10, then up to 50, aiming for at least five per sector. Participants come through referral and outreach, so the sample is not random, and every output says so. The workflow must run at least weekly, involve at least two people, and come from a fixed list: onboarding, invoice approval, purchase requests, sales follow up, complaints, service requests, scheduling, reporting, document approval, supplier onboarding or compliance evidence.

Participants choose a workflow they run regularly, not the one they dislike most. Asking for the worst would inflate every score.

Data captured

From the interview map: steps, handoffs, systems, re entries and waiting points Primary. From the participant: cases per month, lead time, working minutes, and minutes spent chasing, searching, re entering and reworking Estimate. Answers to the 20 diagnostic questions, scored by the researcher from interview evidence Primary.

Scoring

Each answer sits on a four point anchored scale mapped to 0, 33.3, 66.7 and 100. A dimension is the mean of its questions. The Hidden Work Score is the mean of seven dimensions. AI Value Readiness is the mean of six, reversed so higher means more ready. Rounding happens only at the end. Anchored scales mean a score describes the same thing in the first workflow and the fiftieth; normalising against the sample would make every score depend on who else took part.

Bands: 0 to 24 low, 25 to 49 moderate, 50 to 74 high, 75 to 100 severe. The matrix splits both axes at 50.

Measures kept outside the score

Flow efficiency is working minutes divided by lead time in working minutes. Annual hidden effort is hidden minutes per case × cases per month × 12 ÷ 60, shown from 75% to 125%. Waiting costs elapsed time, not labour, so it appears in lead time and flow efficiency but never in hours released. Value is reported in five separate parts: cash, capacity, time avoided, risk and service. Capacity becomes cash only if the time is redeployed or a cost is avoided.

Missing data

A dimension needs at least one answered question. A score needs at least six of seven dimensions, otherwise it isn't published. Nothing is imputed.

Validation plan

Question testing in the first three interviews. A second practitioner scores three interviews blind, aiming for 80% of answers within one point. Scores are recomputed with practitioner weights and with each dimension left out; if no more than one workflow in ten changes quadrant, equal weights stay. From 20 workflows, the score should rise as flow efficiency falls (Spearman correlation); below 0.3 means the dimensions need rethinking. Dimensions correlating above 0.8 are merged.

Ethics and privacy

Voluntary, with consent and the right to withdraw before publication. No customer names, employee names or documents. Organisations are coded unless they agree in writing to be named. Groups under five workflows are never reported. Notes are kept encrypted and deleted 12 months after publication. Independent of any university or employer.

Limitations

The sample is small and not random. Times are estimates, so hours are ranges. One workflow says nothing about the rest of an organisation. Weights are judgement until tested. The researcher interviews and scores, which is why a second rater checks a sample.

The approach builds on established ideas: Hammer's “Reengineering Work: Don't Automate, Obliterate” (Harvard Business Review, 1990), the Lean idea of waste, and flow efficiency. What is new here is applying them together, with an explicit AI suitability test, to Irish workflows in the open.
